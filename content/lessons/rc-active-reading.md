# Reading a CAT Passage

> Every one of the 16 RC questions in CAT (two-thirds of the VARC section) is really a test of one thing: did you build a map of the passage while reading it? Readers who only "take in information" re-read for every question and run out of time; readers who track structure answer most questions from memory and check the passage only to confirm.

## Core ideas

### A CAT passage is an argument, not information

CAT passages are cut from essays, book reviews, long-form journalism and academic prose. They are almost never neutral explanations. Somebody is making a case: that a popular view is wrong, that a book is overrated, that a historical change was caused by something other than what everyone assumes. Your first job is to find that case.

Define the three things you must know by the end of the first read:

- **Subject**: what the passage is about (e.g. the history of clock time).
- **Position**: what the author claims about the subject (e.g. clock time was imposed, not adopted).
- **Opposition**: the view the author is pushing against (e.g. the belief that clocks were a neutral convenience).

If you can state all three in one sentence each, you have a map. If you cannot, you have only read words.

### Paragraph roles

Each paragraph does a job. There are only a handful of jobs, so learn to label them:

| Role | What it does | Typical signal |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | Introduces the subject or the popular view | "It is commonly held that…", "For decades, historians…" |
| Claim | States the author's position | "But this misreads…", "I want to suggest…", "In fact…" |
| Support | Evidence, example, reasoning for the claim | "Consider…", "For instance…", dates, studies |
| Concession | Admits something the other side gets right | "To be sure…", "Admittedly…", "It is true that…" |
| Rebuttal | Takes back the concession, limits it | "But this does not mean…", "Yet…", "Even so…" |
| Qualification | Narrows or refines the claim | "This is not to say…", "Only in…" |
| Implication | Says why it matters, extends the claim | "If this is right, then…", "The lesson is…" |

A typical four-paragraph CAT passage is: Setup → Claim + Support → Concession + Rebuttal → Implication. Once you expect this shape, you read faster because you know what to look for.

### Whose voice is this?

The single most common cause of wrong answers in RC is attributing a sentence to the author when the author is reporting someone else's view. Watch for **reported voice**:

- "Critics argue that…", "Economists have long assumed…", "On this view…"
- "One might think…", "It is tempting to conclude…"

A sentence in reported voice is usually there to be knocked down. The author's own voice arrives with a contrast word: *but, however, yet, nevertheless, in fact*. When you see "Some say X. But Y", the author's view is Y, and any option built on X is a trap.

### Signposts

Words that tell you the direction of the argument. Learn them as a reader learns road signs:

- **Contrast / turn**: but, however, yet, although, whereas, nonetheless, on the contrary → the author is about to disagree or shift.
- **Concession**: admittedly, granted, of course, to be sure, no doubt → the author gives ground; the next "but" takes it back.
- **Cause / consequence**: because, hence, therefore, thus, as a result, it follows → reasoning you can be asked to reproduce.
- **Example**: for instance, consider, take the case of → supporting detail, rarely the main idea.
- **Emphasis**: indeed, crucially, above all, what matters → a sentence the author wants you to remember, often the main claim.

### The 30-second map

After reading, before touching the questions, spend 30 seconds writing (mentally or in two words per paragraph on your rough sheet):

1. One phrase per paragraph naming its role and content: "P1 setup: popular view of clocks. P2 claim: clocks imposed by factory owners. P3 concession: workers also wanted clocks; rebuttal: for different reasons. P4 implication: 'natural' rhythm is historical."
2. The author's position in one line.
3. What the author concedes, in one line. Concessions are question magnets because they let examiners write options that are *half* right.

### How to actually read

- Read once, properly, at a steady pace of about 150–200 words per minute. A 500-word passage should take 2.5–3.5 minutes. Skimming saves a minute and costs two questions.
- Do not re-read a sentence you half understood. Keep going; the next sentence usually explains it. Come back only if a question needs it.
- Underline (mentally) only the turns: *but, however, yet*. Everything before a turn is setup; everything after is where the author lives.
- Do not read the questions first. CAT questions are not keyword-locatable; they test the whole argument. Reading questions first makes you hunt for words instead of understanding the case.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: labelling roles

Read this original mini-passage.

> (P1) It is usually said that the printing press democratised knowledge: once books were cheap, anyone could learn. (P2) The claim is half true. Printing did multiply copies, but it also multiplied the authority of whatever got printed. A manuscript error lived in one library; a printed error lived in a thousand. (P3) Admittedly, errors could now be corrected in a second edition, and a culture of correction did emerge. But correction requires readers who know enough to doubt, and early print culture produced far more readers than doubters. (P4) The press did not so much democratise knowledge as democratise confidence.

**Map.** P1 setup: popular view (press democratised knowledge). P2 claim: half true; printing also multiplied error and authority. P3 concession: corrections possible; rebuttal: correction needs doubters, which print did not create. P4 implication: press democratised confidence, not knowledge.

**Position in one line.** The press spread belief faster than it spread the capacity to judge belief.

**Why this method.** Any question on this passage, whether main idea, tone or inference, is answerable from those four lines. "The author would agree that cheap books made people more knowledgeable" is now obviously wrong (that is P1, the view being attacked), even though the words "cheap" and "knowledge" appear in the passage.

### Example 2: catching reported voice

> Many economists treat the standard working day as a natural unit, as if eight hours were written into human physiology. On this view, labour laws merely recognised a rhythm that already existed. Yet the eight-hour day was fought for, lost, and fought for again over half a century, and its defenders did not appeal to physiology but to the idea that a worker was also a citizen.

**Question.** Which of the following does the author believe? (a) Eight hours is the physiologically natural length of a working day. (b) The eight-hour day was the outcome of political struggle rather than natural rhythm.

**Solution.** Sentence 1 and 2 are reported voice ("Many economists treat…", "On this view…"). "Yet" marks the author's turn. The author's view is what follows: the eight-hour day was fought for, and defended on civic, not physiological, grounds. Answer (b). Option (a) is the reported view, placed there to be rejected.

**Why this method.** The trap option uses the passage's own words. Only tracking the voice tells you that those words belong to the opponent.

### Example 3: using the concession

> No one denies that peer review catches real mistakes; a reviewer who spots a flawed control has done the field a service. But the system was built to filter error, not fraud, and fraud is precisely the case in which the submitted data look clean.

**Question.** The author's attitude to peer review is best described as: (a) dismissive, (b) qualified approval that identifies a specific limitation, (c) wholly supportive, (d) alarmed.

**Solution.** "No one denies… has done the field a service" is a concession: the author grants that peer review works for one purpose. "But" introduces the limit: it was not built for fraud. So the attitude is neither dismissive (the author credits it) nor wholly supportive (the author limits it). Answer (b).

**Why this method.** The concession–rebuttal pair is the author's exact position in miniature. Tone and main-idea options that ignore either half are wrong.

### Example 4: reading at the right speed

Take any 500-word editorial. Time yourself reading it once, without re-reading, then write the four-line map from memory. If it took under two minutes, you skimmed and your map will have gaps; if it took over four, you were re-reading. Practise until a 500-word passage reliably takes about three minutes and the map is complete. This is the single highest-return drill in VARC.

**Why this method.** Speed in RC comes from not going back, not from reading faster. The map is what lets you not go back.

### Example 5: when the author never says "I"

> The fashion for describing cities as "ecosystems" is instructive. Ecosystems have no planners, and the metaphor quietly absolves anyone of responsibility for how a city turns out. A slum, on this reading, is simply a habitat. Those who use the word rarely live in one.

There is no "I argue", but the position is unmistakable: the author disapproves of the ecosystem metaphor because it hides responsibility. The signals are evaluative words ("quietly absolves", "simply a habitat") and the final barbed sentence. Map: Subject: the city-as-ecosystem metaphor. Position: it is a way of dodging responsibility. Opposition: those who use the metaphor.

**Why this method.** In sophisticated prose the position is carried by word choice and placement, not by first-person statements. Reading for evaluative language is part of reading for structure.

## Traps & speed tips

- **Keyword hunting.** Options are written to reuse passage words in the wrong frame. A map beats a word search.
- **Treating the first paragraph as the thesis.** In CAT the first paragraph is often the view to be rejected. The thesis usually arrives with the first "but".
- **Over-marking.** If you underline everything, you have underlined nothing. Mark turns and concessions only.
- **Reading questions first.** Do not. You will read the passage as a lookup table instead of an argument.
- **Panic re-reading.** If a sentence is opaque, note it as "hard sentence, P3" and move on. The questions usually do not depend on it; if one does, you now know where to look.
- **Passage selection.** In the exam, spend 20 seconds on each passage's opening lines and pick the two with the clearest argumentative shape first. Leave the densest for last; attempt it only if time allows.

## Checklist

Before moving to question-type lessons, you should be able to:

- State subject, position and opposition of any 500-word argumentative passage in three lines.
- Label each paragraph with one of the seven roles.
- Identify reported voice and locate the sentence where the author's own voice begins.
- Name the concession the author makes and the rebuttal that follows it.
- Read a 500-word passage once in about three minutes and write the map without looking back.
