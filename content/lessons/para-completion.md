# Para-completion

> One or two questions a year give a short paragraph with a sentence missing, usually the last, and four candidate sentences. The right one must fit three ways at once: it must follow logically from what precedes, match the author's tone and stance, and satisfy any reference (a pronoun, a "this", a "such") in the surrounding text. CAT writes the wrong options to pass one or two of those tests and fail the third.

## Core ideas

### The three fits

1. **Logic.** The missing sentence continues the argument. If the gap is at the end, the sentence concludes, extends or applies the claim; it does not introduce a new topic. If the gap is in the middle, it must link the sentence before to the sentence after: the sentence after must still make sense with the candidate in place.

2. **Tone and stance.** The sentence takes the author's side with the author's intensity. An approving paragraph does not end with a sneer; a measured paragraph does not end with "obviously". If the author has been critical throughout, a sudden endorsement is wrong unless a "but" in the paragraph has prepared it.

3. **Reference.** Any pronoun, "the", "this", "such", "these", "the former/latter" in the candidate must have an antecedent in the paragraph; any such word in the sentence *after* the gap must find its antecedent in the candidate or earlier. Candidates that refer to something not in the paragraph are out.

### The gap at the end

Most CAT gaps are terminal. The closing sentence of an argumentative paragraph typically does one of:

- draws the conclusion the paragraph has been building to ("the lesson is…");
- extends the claim to a new case ("the same is true of…");
- turns the paragraph's point back on the reader or the subject with a twist;
- states the implication for practice, *if* the paragraph has been heading there.

It rarely introduces a brand-new consideration, restates the first sentence verbatim, or hedges back after the author has committed.

### The gap in the middle

Here you have two constraints: what comes before and what comes after. The candidate must be a bridge. Read the sentence after the gap first and note what it needs: a "this", a "however", a "such cases". The candidate must supply it.

### Procedure

1. Read the paragraph, skipping the gap. State the claim and the direction (for, against, mixed).
2. Note what the sentence before the gap ends on and what the sentence after (if any) begins with.
3. Predict, in your own words, what the missing sentence should do (conclude? give the reason? extend?).
4. Test each candidate for the three fits. Eliminate the ones that fail any one.
5. Read the paragraph with the survivor inserted. It must read as a single paragraph.

### Typical wrong options

- **New topic.** Grammatically fine, same subject area, but introduces something the paragraph has not set up.
- **Reversal without a turn.** Contradicts the paragraph's stance with no "but" to license it.
- **Repetition.** Says what the paragraph already said, usually the opening sentence rephrased. Paragraphs do not end by restarting.
- **Overshoot.** Draws a conclusion far beyond the paragraph's argument.
- **Right idea, dangling reference.** Begins with "this" or "such" where there is nothing for it to pick up, or uses a noun the paragraph never mentioned as though it had.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: terminal gap, conclusion

> Forecasts are judged by whether they came true, which is the one standard that tells you nothing about whether they were good. A forecast of 30 per cent rain is not wrong when it rains; it is wrong only if, over many such forecasts, it rains far more or less often than 30 per cent of the time. ______

**Options.** (a) Weather forecasting improved sharply with satellite imagery. (b) It follows that a single forecast can never be evaluated; only a forecaster can. (c) Forecasts are therefore usually accurate. (d) This is why most people distrust forecasters.

**Solution.** Claim: judging forecasts by single outcomes is wrong; judge over many. Predicted ending: the consequence of that. (a) new topic. (c) reversal: the paragraph is about evaluation, not accuracy, and "therefore" does not follow. (d) adds a claim about distrust the paragraph never raises. (b) draws the exact consequence ("over many such forecasts" → only forecasters, not forecasts, can be evaluated). Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Predicting the function of the missing sentence before reading options turns four candidates into one candidate plus three distractions.

### Example 2: tone fit

> The report is careful, thorough and almost unreadable. Its authors have anticipated every objection and answered each in a footnote, so that the argument proceeds through the footnotes while the main text merely gestures at them. ______

**Options.** (a) It is, in short, worthless. (b) The result is a document that will be cited by everyone and read by no one. (c) Thorough reports are always worth the effort. (d) The authors should be congratulated on their rigour.

**Solution.** Tone: wry, mixed; the author respects the care and mocks the result. (a) too harsh: "careful, thorough" has been granted. (d) too warm: "unreadable" has been said. (c) a generalisation that ignores the complaint. (b) matches the mixed, wry tone and concludes the thought about the main text gesturing. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** When logic admits more than one candidate, tone decides. The paragraph has granted merit and complained; the ending must do both.

### Example 3: middle gap with a reference after it

> Museums say they preserve objects for the future. ______ The collector who filled European museums with Egyptian stone was not saving Egypt for posterity; he was deciding that posterity would meet Egypt in London.

**Options.** (a) Many objects would have been lost without collecting. (b) But preservation is a choice about what the future will be allowed to see, made in the present by people with present interests. (c) Egypt has long sought the return of its antiquities. (d) Preservation techniques have improved enormously.

**Solution.** The sentence after the gap gives an example of someone "deciding" what posterity sees. The candidate must set up that idea and turn against the museum's self-description. (b) does both: "But" turns, "a choice… made in the present" is what the collector example illustrates. (a) supports the museums' view and leaves the example unmotivated. (c) and (d) are new topics. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** With a middle gap, the sentence after is the stronger constraint. Here it needs a general claim about deciding for the future; only (b) supplies it.

### Example 4: dangling reference

> Scientists rarely say a theory is true; they say it has not yet been refuted. Outsiders take this for modesty. ______

**Options.** (a) Such refutations are rare in mature sciences. (b) It is the opposite: a claim to a kind of certainty that mere truth could never provide, since what has survived every test has a track record, and truth has only itself. (c) This modesty is one of science's most attractive features. (d) The distinction between truth and refutation was first drawn by philosophers.

**Solution.** Predicted ending: the author corrects the "modesty" reading (the "take this for" phrasing signals the outsiders are wrong). (c) accepts the modesty reading: reversal of the author's implied stance. (a) picks up "refuted" but takes the paragraph nowhere. (d) new topic. (b) corrects the reading and explains. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** "Outsiders take this for modesty" is a set-up for a correction. Reading the author's stance from a reporting phrase is what identifies the right ending.

### Example 5: the repetition trap

> Committees are blamed for slowness, but slowness is what they are for. A decision one person could take in an hour is given to a committee precisely so that it will take a month, during which those affected can discover what is being decided and object. ______

**Options.** (a) Committees are, in other words, slow. (b) Abolish the committee and you do not get the same decision faster; you get a different decision, taken before anyone could ask who it was for. (c) Committees have many members. (d) Slowness, however, is inefficient.

**Solution.** (a) restates the opening without the point: repetition. (c) new topic. (d) reversal without warrant; the paragraph has defended slowness. (b) extends the claim to its consequence (what you lose by abolishing committees). Answer (b).

**Why this method.** A closing sentence must add; an option that merely repeats, however safe it sounds, is wrong.

## Traps & speed tips

- **Reading candidates first.** Predict the function of the gap first; otherwise every candidate sounds possible.
- **Subject-area match.** All four candidates are on topic. That is not evidence.
- **Ignoring the sentence after a middle gap.** It is the strongest clue you have.
- **Endorsing after criticising.** No "but", no reversal.
- **"This"/"such" with no antecedent.** Instant elimination.
- **Time.** Sixty to ninety seconds. Predict (20 s), test (40 s), read through (15 s).

## Checklist

- State the claim and stance of a paragraph with a gap in it.
- Predict the function of the missing sentence before reading options.
- Test each candidate for logic, tone and reference.
- Use the sentence after a middle gap as the primary constraint.
- Recognise new-topic, reversal, repetition, overshoot and dangling-reference options.
