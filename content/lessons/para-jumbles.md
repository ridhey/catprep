# Para-jumbles

> CAT sets two or three para-jumbles a year as TITA (type-in-the-answer) questions: four or five sentences in scrambled order, and you type the digit string of the correct order, such as 4132. There is no negative marking, so every para-jumble should be attempted, and there is no partial credit, so the order must be exactly right. The method is to find mandatory links first and the opening sentence second.

## Core ideas

### What a para-jumble actually is

The sentences are a real paragraph of abstract non-fiction, cut and shuffled. A paragraph has a shape: it introduces a subject, makes a claim, develops it, and closes. The sentences carry evidence of their original position: pronouns that need antecedents, connectors that need something to connect to, examples that need a claim to illustrate. You are reconstructing the shape from that evidence.

### Mandatory pairs

A **mandatory pair** is two sentences that must be adjacent in a fixed order. Finding one or two pairs usually fixes the whole order. The evidence:

1. **Pronoun reference.** "He", "she", "they", "it", "this", "these", "such" need a noun earlier. "This objection" must follow the sentence that states an objection. "They" must follow the sentence that names a plural noun. The sentence with the full noun comes first; the sentence with the pronoun comes second.

2. **Definite reference.** "The experiment", "the second reason", "the same logic" presuppose that the experiment, a first reason, or the logic has already appeared. "A" or "an" introduces; "the" refers back.

3. **Connectors.** *However, but, yet, nevertheless* follow a sentence they contrast with. *Therefore, hence, thus, so* follow a reason. *Moreover, also, further, similarly* follow a point of the same kind. *For instance, for example* follow a general claim. A sentence beginning with a connector is never first.

4. **Chronology.** Dates, "first… then… finally", "earlier… later", "at the time… since then". Time runs forward unless a connector signals a flashback.

5. **General to specific.** A claim precedes its example; a definition precedes its application; a category precedes its members.

6. **Question and answer.** A sentence posing a question is followed by the one that answers or addresses it.

7. **Repeated noun with elaboration.** Sentence A mentions "a paradox"; sentence B says "The paradox is this: …". A then B.

### The opening sentence

The opener introduces the subject with full nouns, no backward-pointing pronouns, no contrast connector, and usually a general statement rather than a detail. Candidates for opener: the sentence that would make sense if it were the only sentence you read. If two sentences qualify, use the pairs to decide; the opener is the one that nothing else needs to precede.

### The closing sentence

The closer draws a conclusion ("the lesson is", "in the end"), extends the claim ("the same is true of…"), or returns to the opening with a twist. It is the sentence that nothing else needs to follow.

### Procedure

1. Read all sentences once, quickly, for subject.
2. Mark every pronoun, "the"-phrase and connector. For each, find the sentence that supplies what it needs. These are your pairs.
3. Chain the pairs. If A–B and B–C, you have A–B–C.
4. Place the remaining sentence(s) by opener/closer tests.
5. **Read the paragraph in your order, start to finish.** It must read as one argument with no jolt. If it jolts, the jolt is at the wrong link.
6. Type the digits with no spaces.

### Unique order

CAT para-jumbles have exactly one defensible order. If you think two orders work, you have missed a link: go back to the pronouns and connectors. Usually one of your orders leaves a pronoun without an antecedent or puts "however" after a sentence it does not contrast with.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: pronoun and connector

> 1. This confidence is misplaced, because the categories were built from the very cases they are now asked to explain.
> 2. Historians sort revolutions into types: the liberal, the nationalist, the peasant.
> 3. The sorting is useful for teaching, and it gives the field a confident vocabulary.
> 4. A category that was drawn around three examples will of course fit those three examples; whether it fits a fourth is the only interesting question.

**Solution.** Sentence 1 begins "This confidence", so it must follow a sentence that mentions confidence: sentence 3 ("confident vocabulary"). Pair: 3–1. Sentence 3 begins "The sorting", which refers back to sentence 2's "sort". Pair: 2–3. Sentence 4 explains why the categories "built from the very cases" cannot fail: it elaborates sentence 1. Chain: 2–3–1–4. Check the opener: sentence 2 has full nouns and no backward reference. Read through: sorting, its usefulness and confidence, the confidence misplaced, the reason. Answer: **2314**.

**Why this method.** Two reference links (3→1 by "this confidence", 2→3 by "the sorting") fix three of four sentences; the fourth has only one place to go.

### Example 2: chronology against a flashback

> 1. By the 1950s it had become the standard account, taught in every textbook and questioned in none.
> 2. The theory was first proposed in 1911, to general indifference.
> 3. Its acceptance owed less to new evidence than to the retirement of the generation that had ignored it.
> 4. What changed between those dates was not the theory but the profession.

**Solution.** Sentence 2 introduces "the theory" with a date; sentence 1 continues with "it" and a later date (1950s). Pair: 2–1. Sentence 4 says "between those dates", which needs two dates already given: so 4 follows 1. Sentence 3 explains "what changed… the profession" (retirement of a generation): it elaborates 4. Chain: 2–1–4–3. Answer: **2143**.

**Why this method.** "Those dates" is a plural definite reference that needs both dates before it; that single phrase forbids any order putting 4 before 1.

### Example 3: the "however" that cannot start

> 1. However, the same study found that the effect vanished when participants were told that the test was timed.
> 2. The effect is real but fragile, and anything fragile should be handled with care before it is built into policy.
> 3. A widely cited study found that people who were reminded of their own competence performed better on a subsequent test.
> 4. Policymakers took the first finding and left the second.

**Solution.** Sentence 1 ("However, the same study…") contrasts with a study already mentioned: follows 3. Pair: 3–1. Sentence 4 refers to "the first finding" and "the second": needs both findings before it, so 4 follows 1. Sentence 2 gives the author's conclusion about the effect ("real but fragile") and about policy. Should 2 come before or after 4? If 2 came before 4, "the first finding and left the second" would still work, but the paragraph would end on the policymakers' error without a point; if 2 comes last, the author draws the lesson from the error. Also 2's "before it is built into policy" responds to 4's "policymakers". Chain: 3–1–4–2. Answer: **3142**.

**Why this method.** When a sentence is the author's general lesson and another is the specific failure that prompted it, the lesson closes. Place the verdict last.

### Example 4: five sentences, general to specific

> 1. The word "efficient" does similar work: it tells you that a cost was cut, not whose cost it was.
> 2. Such words are not lies; they are instructions about where not to look.
> 3. Bureaucracies have a vocabulary for making decisions sound like events.
> 4. A "restructuring" is a decision to dismiss people, described as weather.
> 5. The vocabulary is worth studying because its purpose is to go unnoticed.

**Solution.** Sentence 3 introduces "a vocabulary" (indefinite). Sentence 5 says "The vocabulary" (definite) and gives a reason to study it: 3–5. Sentence 4 gives the first example ("a restructuring"). Sentence 1 says "does similar work", which needs a previous example: 4–1. Sentence 2 says "Such words", needing the examples before it, and draws the general lesson: closer. Chain: 3–5–4–1–2. Could 5 go after the examples, before 2? "Its purpose is to go unnoticed" would then sit awkwardly before "such words are not lies"; and 5's "worth studying because" is a reason to proceed to examples, not a conclusion. Answer: **35412**.

**Why this method.** Two examples with "similar work" linking them, an introduction with "a/the" linking, and a "such" closer: three independent links agree on one order.

### Example 5: opener by elimination

> 1. It is easy to mistake this for modesty.
> 2. Scientists rarely say a theory is true; they say it has not yet been refuted.
> 3. In fact it is the opposite: a claim to a kind of certainty that mere truth could never provide, since what has survived every test so far has a track record, and truth has only itself.
> 4. The habit puzzles outsiders, who expect a profession devoted to knowledge to be willing to say it knows.

**Solution.** Opener candidates: 2 (full nouns, general claim) and 4 ("The habit" is definite, so it refers back: not an opener). Sentence 1 ("this") needs the habit; sentence 3 ("In fact it is the opposite") corrects sentence 1's "modesty". Pairs: 1–3, and 4 follows 2 ("the habit" = rarely saying true). Does 1 follow 2 or 4? "Mistake this for modesty": the habit of not claiming truth, which 4 has just framed as puzzling. 2–4–1–3 reads: habit, outsiders puzzled, easy to call it modesty, in fact the opposite. Answer: **2413**.

**Why this method.** Definite references ("the habit", "this") rule out openers as reliably as connectors do.

## Traps & speed tips

- **Starting with the opener.** Beginners hunt for the first sentence; experts hunt for pairs. Pairs are objective; openers are a judgement.
- **Ignoring "the".** The difference between "a theory" and "the theory" places two sentences.
- **Connector that fits two places.** "However" can contrast with more than one sentence. Choose the one where the contrast is direct (same subject, opposite claim).
- **Forgetting to read through.** A chain built from links can still jolt. The read-through catches a wrong link in ten seconds.
- **Typing errors.** Write the order on paper, then type. Check digit count equals sentence count.
- **Time.** Two to three minutes per question. If after three minutes two orders still seem possible, pick the one with more satisfied links and move on; no negative marking.

## Checklist

- Identify pronoun, definite-reference and connector links and turn each into an ordered pair.
- Chain pairs into a full order and place leftovers by opener/closer tests.
- Explain why a given order is the only defensible one by naming each link.
- Read the final paragraph through and detect a jolt.
- Complete a five-sentence jumble in under three minutes.
