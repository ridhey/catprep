# Elimination & Option Analysis

> Every RC question is four options with one answer, and CAT's option writers use a small repertoire to make the three wrong ones tempting. Knowing the repertoire turns the question around: instead of looking for the right answer, you find three provable flaws and keep what remains. At 99th-percentile accuracy, almost every decision between the last two options is made this way.

## Core ideas

### The four flaws

Every wrong RC option is wrong in one of four ways. Name the flaw and you have earned the right to eliminate.

1. **Extreme.** The option says more than the passage does: *all, never, only, always, entirely, proves, the sole cause, impossible, must*. The passage hedged (*many, often, tends to, suggests*); the option does not. Test: find the quantifier or modal verb in the option and ask whether the passage licenses it.

2. **Out of scope.** The option introduces something the passage does not discuss: a recommendation the author never makes, a comparison the author never draws, a group or time period the passage never covers. It may be true in the world; that is irrelevant. Test: can you point to the sentence that supports this? If the only support is your general knowledge, the option is out of scope.

3. **Distortion.** The option uses the passage's material but changes the claim: swaps cause and effect, replaces "necessary" with "sufficient", changes who did what, turns a concession into the main point, turns an example into a thesis, or shifts the degree ("reduced" becomes "eliminated"). Test: compare the option clause by clause with the sentence it is built from; one clause will differ.

4. **Opposite.** The option states the view the author rejects, or negates the author's view. Often it is a paraphrase of the opening paragraph (the "popular view") or of a reported voice. Test: does this match the author's position line from your map, or the opposition line?

A fifth label, **half-right**, is a sub-type of distortion: the option is a compound ("X and Y", "X because Y") in which X is true and Y is false. CAT writers love this because a reader who confirms X stops reading.

### The correct option's disguise

The correct option is usually the *least* striking of the four. It paraphrases rather than quotes, hedges where the passage hedges, and often sounds flat. Readers who pick the "most impressive" option pick the extreme or out-of-scope one. Expect the answer to be modest.

### A procedure for the last two

When two options survive:

1. Find the **difference**. Write (mentally) what one says that the other does not. Usually one word: a quantifier, a verb, a scope term.
2. **Locate** the passage sentence that decides the difference.
3. The option that goes beyond that sentence loses.

If you cannot find a difference, re-read both: one is probably a subset of the other, and the narrower one is right for detail and inference questions, while the broader one is right for main-idea questions only if it still stays within the passage.

### Keyword density as a warning

Count how many of the option's words appear verbatim in the passage. High density with a different meaning is the signature of distortion. Low density with the same meaning is the signature of a correct paraphrase. This is not a rule, but it is a useful suspicion.

### When to leave a question

If after elimination two options remain and you cannot locate a deciding sentence within 30 seconds, mark your best guess, note it, and move on. Negative marking punishes a 50–50 guess less than it punishes the two questions you will not reach. But do not leave a question with three options eliminated; that is a question you have answered.

## Worked examples

For all examples below, use this original passage.

> It is widely believed that the printing press democratised knowledge. The belief is half true. Printing multiplied copies, but it also multiplied the authority of whatever got printed: a manuscript error lived in one library, a printed error in a thousand. Admittedly, errors could now be corrected in later editions, and a culture of correction did emerge. But correction requires readers who know enough to doubt, and early print culture produced many more readers than doubters. The press did not so much democratise knowledge as democratise confidence.

### Example 1: name the flaw in each option

**The author's main point is that:** (a) the printing press made no contribution to the spread of knowledge. (b) printing spread confident belief faster than it spread the ability to judge belief. (c) manuscripts were more accurate than printed books. (d) the printing press should have been regulated to prevent the spread of error.

**Solution.** (a) Extreme and opposite: "half true" concedes a contribution. (c) Distortion: the passage says a manuscript error reached fewer people, not that manuscripts were more accurate. (d) Out of scope: no recommendation about regulation anywhere. (b) is the modest paraphrase of the last sentence, covering the whole argument. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Naming the flaw is faster and more reliable than arguing for the right answer, because each flaw has a one-word test.

### Example 2: the half-right option

**Which of the following can be inferred?** (a) Later editions sometimes corrected errors, and most early readers were capable of spotting them. (b) The author believes the culture of correction was an illusion. (c) The multiplication of copies increased the reach of both truth and error. (d) Early readers were more credulous than manuscript readers had been.

**Solution.** (a) is half-right: the first clause is conceded by the passage; the second contradicts "many more readers than doubters". Eliminate. (b) is opposite: the author says a culture of correction "did emerge". (d) is a comparison the passage never makes (early print readers vs earlier manuscript readers); out of scope. (c) is forced by "multiplied copies… multiplied the authority of whatever got printed", which applies to truth and error alike. Answer (c).

**Why this method.** Read every clause of a compound option. The second clause is where the trap is set.

### Example 3: last two options

**The author's attitude to the claim that printing democratised knowledge is:** (a) complete rejection. (b) acceptance with a significant qualification. (c) acceptance. (d) qualified acceptance that the claim understates printing's benefits.

**Solution.** (a) and (c) go quickly: "half true" is neither rejection nor acceptance. Between (b) and (d): the difference is the direction of the qualification. (d) says the claim understates benefits; (b) says it is qualified. The passage's qualification is that printing also spread error and confidence, which is a *limitation* on the claim, not an understatement of benefits. (d) is a distortion. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Locate the single difference, find the deciding sentence, and let it choose. Here the deciding material is the second half of the passage, which is entirely about costs.

### Example 4: keyword density

**It can be inferred that the author regards "readers who know enough to doubt" as:** (a) the product of print culture, which multiplied readers and so multiplied doubters. (b) a precondition for correction that early print culture did not supply in proportion to its readers. (c) a danger to the authority of printed books. (d) the main beneficiaries of the printing press.

**Solution.** (a) reuses "print culture", "multiplied", "readers" and inverts the meaning: the passage says print produced readers, *not* doubters in proportion. Distortion with high keyword density. (c) and (d) are out of scope (no danger, no beneficiaries discussed). (b) paraphrases "correction requires readers who know enough to doubt… many more readers than doubters" without reusing the key words. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** The suspiciously familiar option was wrong; the unfamiliar paraphrase was right. That is the usual pattern.

### Example 5: the modest answer

**Which statement would the author most likely endorse?** (a) Technology always has unintended consequences. (b) The spread of a medium is not the same as the spread of the judgement needed to use it. (c) Printing was, on balance, harmful. (d) Confidence is more dangerous than ignorance.

**Solution.** (a) is too broad: "technology always" exceeds a passage about one medium. (c) is extreme: the author says "half true", not "harmful on balance". (d) is a quotable aphorism that the passage never states; out of scope. (b) is the principle behind the passage, stated at the passage's own level of generality. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Two options are grander than the passage and one is darker. The one that sounds like the passage wins.

## Traps & speed tips

- **Confirming instead of eliminating.** If you read an option looking for reasons it is right, you will find them. Read looking for the flaw.
- **The impressive option.** Grand, quotable options are usually extreme or out of scope.
- **Stopping at the first true clause.** Compound options must be true in every clause.
- **Eliminating the flat option.** The correct option often sounds dull because it is a careful paraphrase. Do not penalise it for that.
- **Spending equal time on all four.** Two options usually fall in five seconds each. Spend your time on the last two.
- **Changing an answer without a reason.** Change only when you have found the deciding sentence. "It felt wrong" is not a reason.

## Checklist

- Name the flaw (extreme, out of scope, distortion, opposite, half-right) of any wrong option in a sentence.
- Run the quantifier test, the support test and the clause-by-clause comparison from memory.
- Resolve a last-two decision by locating the single difference and the deciding sentence.
- Treat high keyword density as a warning and flat paraphrase as a good sign.
- Decide in under 30 seconds whether to commit or move on.
