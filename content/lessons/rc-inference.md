# Inference Questions

> "Which of the following can be inferred from the passage?", "The author would most likely agree that…", "It follows from the passage that…": inference questions are 4–6 of the 16 RC questions each year and have the lowest accuracy of any type, because the wrong options are plausible. The method is to replace "sounds right" with "must be true".

## Core ideas

### What an inference is

An **inference** is a statement that is not written in the passage but is forced by what is written. If the passage is true, the inference cannot be false. Everything else, however sensible, is not an inference.

Compare three statements about this sentence: "Every reviewer the journal consulted recommended rejection, yet the paper was published."

- "The journal does not always follow its reviewers." Forced. Reviewers all said reject; it was published; so the journal overrode them at least once. Inference.
- "The editor had a personal interest in the paper." Possible, even likely in a story, but nothing in the sentence rules out other explanations (a policy of publishing controversial work, a mistake). Not an inference.
- "The paper was later retracted." Pure story-telling. Not an inference.

CAT options live in the second category: plausible, consistent with the passage, and not forced by it. Your job is to refuse them.

### The one-step rule

A valid CAT inference is **one step** from the text: take a sentence (or two sentences combined) and draw the immediate consequence. If you find yourself saying "and then, since people usually…, it follows that…", you have taken a second step using something that is not in the passage. Stop. Options that require a second step are wrong even when every step is reasonable.

### The negation test

To check whether an option is an inference, **negate it** and ask whether the negation contradicts the passage.

- Option: "The journal does not always follow its reviewers." Negation: "The journal always follows its reviewers." This contradicts the sentence (they said reject; it was published). So the option is an inference.
- Option: "The editor had a personal interest." Negation: "The editor had no personal interest." Is this contradicted by the passage? No; the passage is silent. So the option is not an inference.

This test takes five seconds per option and removes most traps.

### Three sources of inference in a passage

1. **From a contrast.** "Unlike X, Y does Z" lets you infer "X does not do Z". "Earlier accounts ignored the role of trust" lets you infer "the author's account does not ignore it".
2. **From a concession.** "Admittedly, some objects would have been lost" lets you infer that the author accepts that collecting had at least one benefit. Options that say the author "denies any benefit" are therefore wrong.
3. **From word choice.** "The so-called reform" lets you infer the author doubts it was a reform. "Merely", "simply", "quietly" signal disapproval; "rightly", "crucially" signal approval. These give you the author's attitude, which is what "the author would agree that" questions test.

### "The author would most likely agree"

These are inference questions about the author's position rather than about facts. Use the map: subject, position, opposition. The correct option restates the position, applies it to a case the passage mentions, or denies the opposition. Wrong options: restate the opposition (opposite), apply the position far beyond its scope (too broad), or make the author say something stronger than she did (extreme).

### EXCEPT and "cannot be inferred"

"All of the following can be inferred EXCEPT" reverses the task: three options pass the negation test and one fails. Work through all four; the one you cannot force from the text is the answer. Do not stop at the first option that "seems off"; check that the other three are genuinely forced.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: the forced vs the plausible

> The village had two wells. The deeper one was used only in the dry months, when the shallow one failed, and its water was said to taste of iron. Nobody drank from it willingly.

**Which can be inferred?** (a) The deeper well's water contained iron. (b) In the wet months, villagers drank from the shallow well. (c) The deeper well was dug after the shallow one. (d) The villagers preferred the taste of the shallow well's water.

**Solution.** (a) The passage says the water "was said to taste of iron"; a reputation, not a fact. Negate: "it contained no iron"; not contradicted. Reject. (b) The deep well was used *only* in dry months; the shallow one failed only in dry months; so in wet months the shallow well was the one in use. Negate: "in the wet months they did not drink from the shallow well"; that contradicts "deeper one used only in dry months" (they had two wells and were drinking from one). Inference. (c) Chronology of digging is never mentioned. Reject. (d) Tempting, but "nobody drank from it willingly" tells us they disliked the deep well's water, not that they *preferred the taste* of the other; they might have disliked both. Negate: "they did not prefer the shallow well's taste"; not contradicted. Reject. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** (d) is the classic trap: it fits the story. The negation test is what separates a story that fits from a statement that is forced.

### Example 2: inference from contrast

> Earlier biographies of the composer treated his deafness as a tragedy heroically overcome. The letters tell a less flattering and more interesting story: deafness freed him from the obligation to perform, and performance had been the part of music he liked least.

**The author would most likely agree that:** (a) the composer's deafness was not a tragedy. (b) earlier biographers were unaware of the letters. (c) the composer's late work was better than his early work. (d) the heroic framing of the composer's deafness obscures something about his relationship to music.

**Solution.** (a) is extreme: "less flattering and more interesting story" does not deny tragedy; it adds something. (b) is a second-step guess; the biographers may have read the letters and ignored them. (c) is nowhere in the text. (d) is forced by the contrast: the earlier framing ("tragedy heroically overcome") is set against what the letters reveal (he disliked performing); so the heroic framing missed that. Answer (d).

**Why this method.** The contrast "earlier… / the letters…" is the author's inference engine. Read what the contrast denies, not what you would like it to deny.

### Example 3: inference from a concession

> It would be foolish to deny that targeted advertising works; the firms that buy it are not charities. The question is what "works" means. An advert that sells one more pair of shoes to someone who was already browsing for shoes has worked, in the narrow sense; whether it has changed anything is another matter.

**Which of the following can be inferred?** (a) The author believes targeted advertising is a fraud. (b) The author accepts that targeted advertising increases sales. (c) Firms that buy targeted advertising are wasting money. (d) Targeted advertising never changes anyone's mind.

**Solution.** The first sentence is a concession: it works, in some sense. (b) follows directly from "it would be foolish to deny that it works" plus the shoe example. (a) and (c) contradict the concession. (d) is extreme: "whether it has changed anything is another matter" raises a doubt; it does not assert "never". Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Concessions generate guaranteed inferences (the author grants X) and guaranteed traps (the author denies X). Knowing where the concession is tells you which is which.

### Example 4: EXCEPT

> Unlike the lecture, the seminar presumes that students have already read; it begins where the lecture ends. This is why seminars fail so visibly when reading has not happened: there is nothing to begin from.

**All of the following can be inferred EXCEPT:** (a) Lectures do not presume prior reading. (b) A seminar with unprepared students lacks its starting point. (c) Lectures are less valuable than seminars. (d) The seminar and the lecture differ in what they assume of students.

**Solution.** (a): "Unlike the lecture, the seminar presumes…" forces that the lecture does not presume. Inference. (b): directly restated ("nothing to begin from"). Inference. (d): the whole first sentence. Inference. (c): the passage compares what the two assume and when they begin, not their value. Negate: "lectures are not less valuable"; nothing contradicts it. Not an inference. Answer (c).

**Why this method.** On EXCEPT questions, verifying the three true options is as important as spotting the false one; otherwise you will pick an inference that merely sounded less familiar.

### Example 5: the two-step temptation

> The survey found that respondents who had recently moved house reported lower trust in their neighbours than those who had not.

**Which is a valid inference?** (a) Moving house reduces trust in neighbours. (b) People who trust their neighbours are less likely to move. (c) Recent movers in the survey reported lower neighbourly trust than non-movers. (d) Trust in neighbours rises with time spent in a place.

**Solution.** (a), (b) and (d) are each causal stories that explain the finding. Each requires a step beyond the data (direction of causation; time trend). Only (c) is one step, in fact zero steps: a restatement. In CAT, the correct inference is often this modest. Answer (c).

**Why this method.** Correlation sentences generate three causal traps and one correct restatement. Recognise the pattern and you will never spend more than 30 seconds on it.

## Traps & speed tips

- **Plausibility is not inference.** The most sensible-sounding option is often the trap, because sensible stories require extra assumptions.
- **Strength creep.** "May", "some", "often" in the passage become "will", "all", "always" in the option. Match the hedge.
- **Opposite of the concession.** Any option saying the author "denies", "rejects entirely" or "sees no merit in" something the author conceded is wrong.
- **Scope slide.** The passage talks about seminars at a particular university; the option talks about "education". Reject.
- **Speed.** Do the negation test only on the two options that survive a first look; the other two are usually out of scope and can be dropped in seconds.
- **"Most likely agree" is still an inference.** Use the position line from your map; do not reconstruct the author's whole worldview.

## Checklist

- Explain the difference between forced, plausible and invented statements.
- Apply the negation test to any option in under ten seconds.
- Pull inferences from contrasts, concessions and evaluative word choice.
- Recognise and refuse second-step reasoning.
- Handle EXCEPT questions by verifying all four options.
