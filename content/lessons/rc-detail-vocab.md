# Specific Detail & Vocabulary in Context

> Two or three questions per CAT paper ask what the passage says about a particular point ("According to the passage…", "The author mentions X in order to…") or what a word or phrase means where it is used. They are the easiest RC questions to get right and the most embarrassing to get wrong; the method is locate, read around, paraphrase, then match.

## Core ideas

### Detail questions: locate, then read around

A detail question points at a specific place in the passage. The procedure:

1. **Pick the anchor.** Take the most specific noun or phrase in the question (a name, a technical term, a number). That is what you search for; common words like "the author says" are not anchors.
2. **Locate.** Use your paragraph map: you know which paragraph discussed the anchor. Go there, not to the top of the passage.
3. **Read around.** Read the sentence before and after the anchor. Detail traps are built from the right sentence read without its neighbours: a claim that is immediately qualified, or a view that is reported and then rejected.
4. **Paraphrase, then match.** Say in your own words what the passage says about the anchor. Then find the option that means the same thing. Do not match words; match meaning.

### Why "paraphrase" matters

The correct option almost never repeats the passage's wording; it restates it. The wrong options often *do* reuse the passage's words, rearranged into a different claim. A reader who matches words picks the trap; a reader who matches meaning picks the restatement.

Example: passage says "the reform reduced waiting times but not costs". An option saying "the reform reduced costs and waiting times" reuses every word and reverses the meaning. An option saying "the reform's effect was on delay rather than expense" shares no key words and is correct.

### "The author mentions X in order to"

This is a detail question about function, not content. Ask: what sentence does X support? X is almost always an example, an analogy, or a counter-example, and the answer is the claim it illustrates or the view it undermines. Find the general statement immediately before or after X; that is the purpose.

### "All of the following are mentioned EXCEPT"

Four options, three of which are in the passage. Verify each of the three by locating it; the one you cannot locate is the answer. Beware: the un-mentioned option is often a sensible-sounding statement that *would* fit the passage. "Sensible" is not "mentioned".

### Vocabulary in context

The question quotes a word or phrase and asks what it means "as used in the passage". Three facts:

- The word is chosen because its **ordinary dictionary meaning is not the one in use**, or because it has several meanings. The dictionary meaning is the trap.
- The passage **defines the word by its surroundings**: by a contrast ("not X but Y"), by an example, by apposition ("the ledger, that is, the record of…"), or by what the sentence needs it to mean to make sense.
- The test is **substitution**: put each option into the sentence in place of the word and read the sentence. Only one keeps the sentence meaningful and consistent with the surrounding argument.

Common CAT vocabulary moves:

- A familiar word used technically: "economy" meaning *thrift* ("an economy of style"); "sanction" meaning *permit* rather than *punish*; "appreciate" meaning *rise in value*; "critical" meaning *decisive* rather than *fault-finding*.
- A metaphor: "the argument has no purchase on the facts" (purchase = grip).
- An ironic use: "a generous estimate" meaning *an exaggeration*.
- A phrase whose meaning is set by contrast: "not a map but a compass" (gives direction, not detail).

### What detail questions are not

They are not inference questions. If the option goes one step beyond what is written, it is wrong *for a detail question* even if it would be a valid inference. "According to the passage" means stated, not implied.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: read around the anchor

> The committee recommended a single national examination. Its report conceded that such an examination would favour students from well-resourced schools, and proposed a weighting to offset this; the weighting was dropped before the scheme was implemented.

**According to the passage, the committee:** (a) opposed a national examination because it favoured well-resourced schools. (b) recommended a national examination together with a corrective weighting. (c) implemented a national examination without any weighting. (d) was unaware that the examination would favour some students.

**Solution.** Anchor: "committee". Read all three clauses. The committee recommended the exam (so (a) is opposite), conceded the bias (so (d) is opposite), and proposed a weighting (so (b) is right). The weighting was dropped "before the scheme was implemented" by an unnamed agent, not by the committee; (c) attributes implementation to the committee and that is a distortion. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Three of four options are built from the same sentence. Only reading the whole sentence, including who did what, separates them.

### Example 2: "in order to"

> We expect precision from instruments and forgive its absence in people. A thermometer that reads two degrees high is broken; a friend who is always ten minutes late is merely a friend who is always ten minutes late. This asymmetry explains much of our discomfort with algorithmic decisions: we hold them to the standard of instruments while they make the kinds of errors we associate with people.

**The author mentions the thermometer and the late friend in order to:** (a) argue that people should be as punctual as instruments are precise. (b) illustrate the different standards we apply to instruments and to people. (c) show that algorithms are more like friends than thermometers. (d) criticise people who are habitually late.

**Solution.** The examples sit between a general claim ("we expect precision from instruments and forgive its absence in people") and its application (algorithms). They illustrate the general claim. (a) and (d) turn an illustration into a recommendation or a complaint. (c) distorts: the author says we *treat* algorithms as instruments while they err like people; she does not say they are more like friends. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** An example's purpose is the general sentence it sits next to. Find that sentence and the answer writes itself.

### Example 3: EXCEPT

> The survey asked households about three things: how much they saved each month, whether they expected prices to rise over the next year, and whether they had borrowed from relatives in the past six months. It did not ask about income, which the designers judged too sensitive.

**All of the following were asked about EXCEPT:** (a) monthly saving, (b) expectations of inflation, (c) household income, (d) borrowing from relatives.

**Solution.** Locate each: saving (yes), prices rising (yes; "expectations of inflation" is a paraphrase), borrowing from relatives (yes). Income: explicitly *not* asked. Answer (c). Note that (b) does not use the passage's words; you must recognise the paraphrase.

**Why this method.** Verify by locating; do not rely on memory of what "sounds like" it was there.

### Example 4: vocabulary by contrast

> The report is exhaustive without being exhausting: it covers every relevant case, but the prose carries the reader rather than burying her.

**As used here, "exhaustive" most nearly means:** (a) tiring, (b) comprehensive, (c) excessive, (d) final.

**Solution.** The sentence sets "exhaustive" against "exhausting" and then glosses both: "covers every relevant case" (exhaustive) versus "burying her" (exhausting). So exhaustive = comprehensive. Substitute: "comprehensive without being exhausting" reads correctly. (a) is the meaning of the *other* word. (c) contradicts the praise. (d) is a different sense altogether. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** The sentence's own contrast defines the word. Substitution confirms it.

### Example 5: the dictionary trap

> The committee's findings were damning, and the board's response was to sanction the practice it had been asked to stop.

**"Sanction" here means:** (a) penalise, (b) investigate, (c) authorise, (d) postpone.

**Solution.** Substitute. "The board's response was to penalise the practice it had been asked to stop" would be a reasonable response, and the sentence's "and" after "damning" suggests something unreasonable or surprising. "The board's response was to authorise the practice it had been asked to stop" delivers that surprise: findings were damning, yet the board approved what it was told to stop. "Sanction" has both meanings; the context selects *authorise*. (a) is the dictionary trap. Answer (c).

**Why this method.** Where a word has two opposed meanings, the surrounding logic (here, the implied contrast between damning findings and the board's response) chooses between them.

### Example 6: a metaphor

> Her theory has elegance and ambition, but it finds no purchase on the data: every prediction it makes is either untestable or already known to be false.

**"Purchase" most nearly means:** (a) cost, (b) grip, (c) market, (d) value.

**Solution.** "Finds no purchase on the data" is followed by an explanation: predictions untestable or false. So the theory cannot get a hold on the facts. Substitute "grip": "finds no grip on the data" fits. (a), (c) and (d) are the commercial meanings and make no sense in the sentence. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** When the ordinary meaning produces nonsense, look for the figurative one and let the following clause confirm it.

## Traps & speed tips

- **Word-matching.** The option that reuses the passage's words is suspect; the one that paraphrases is likely.
- **Half a sentence.** Qualifying clauses ("but not costs", "before it was implemented") are where traps are made. Read to the full stop.
- **Wrong agent.** Who did it? Detail options swap the committee for the board, the critic for the author.
- **Dictionary meaning.** For vocab-in-context, the first meaning that comes to mind is usually the trap. Substitute every option.
- **Inference creep.** "According to the passage" means stated. Do not reward yourself for a clever inference on a detail question.
- **Speed.** Detail questions should take 45–60 seconds: ten to locate, twenty to read around, the rest to match. If you are hunting for more than fifteen seconds, your map was incomplete; go to the paragraph whose role fits the question.

## Checklist

- Choose an anchor and locate it using the paragraph map, not a top-down scan.
- Read the sentence before and after the anchor before answering.
- Paraphrase the passage's statement, then match meaning rather than words.
- Identify the purpose of an example by finding the general claim beside it.
- Solve vocabulary-in-context by substitution and reject the dictionary trap.
