# Tone, Attitude & Author's Stance

> One or two questions a year ask directly for the author's tone or attitude; many more depend on it indirectly, because "the author would agree that…" and main-idea options are written to match or mismatch the author's stance. Tone is read from word choice, hedging and the gap between what the author describes and what the author endorses.

## Core ideas

### Tone, attitude, stance

- **Tone** is the emotional colour of the writing: detached, sardonic, admiring, anxious.
- **Attitude** is the author's evaluation of a specific thing in the passage: the author's attitude *toward the reform*, *toward earlier historians*.
- **Stance** is the author's position in the argument: for, against, undecided, partly for.

A passage can have a dispassionate tone and a strongly critical stance; many academic passages do. Questions usually ask about attitude toward something specific, so always identify the object.

### Where tone lives

Tone is almost never stated. It lives in:

1. **Evaluative adjectives and adverbs**: "the so-called reform", "a quietly devastating finding", "a merely technical point", "rightly ignored". *Quietly, merely, simply, so-called, supposedly* are disapproving; *rightly, crucially, admirably, genuinely* are approving.
2. **Verbs of reporting**: "the minister claimed" (doubt) vs "the minister showed" (acceptance) vs "the minister noted" (neutral). "Insists", "assures us", "would have us believe" all signal scepticism.
3. **Hedging**: "it may be that", "perhaps", "seems", "one might suggest" mark a tentative or cautious author. The absence of hedging where you would expect it ("it is obvious that", "nobody seriously believes") marks confidence or polemic.
4. **Irony**: saying the opposite of what is meant, flagged by exaggeration or incongruity. "The committee, with its usual efficiency, took four years." The word "efficiency" is ironic because of "four years".
5. **Placement**: the sentence after a concession, and the last sentence of the passage, carry the author's weight.

### Describing vs endorsing

The most tested distinction. An author who writes "Supporters of the policy argue that it reduced crime" has *described* a view, not *endorsed* it. Endorsement needs the author's own voice: "and they are right", "as the data confirm", or simply the absence of a following "but". Rule: a reported view followed by a turn word is being rejected; a reported view with no turn and with supporting detail from the author is being adopted.

### The intensity scale

Tone words differ by strength. Place the passage before you choose:

| Attitude | Mild | Moderate | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negative | sceptical, reserved, wary | critical, dismissive, disapproving | scathing, contemptuous, hostile |
| Positive | appreciative, sympathetic | admiring, approving | laudatory, celebratory, reverential |
| Neutral / mixed | analytical, dispassionate, detached | ambivalent, equivocal, balanced | — |

CAT authors are usually moderate: critical but not scathing, appreciative but not reverential. A strong tone word in an option is correct only if the passage contains strong language (sarcasm, outright dismissal). If the passage is measured, the measured word wins.

### Tone vocabulary you must know

Define these; CAT uses them in options without explanation.

- **Ambivalent**: holding mixed feelings; sees merit and fault. Not the same as *indifferent* (does not care) or *neutral* (takes no side).
- **Equivocal**: deliberately non-committal, could be read either way.
- **Sceptical**: doubting, demanding evidence. Not *cynical* (assuming bad motives).
- **Sardonic / sarcastic**: mocking, often through irony. *Wry* is gentler, amused.
- **Polemical**: aggressively argumentative, attacking a position.
- **Didactic**: instructing, lecturing the reader.
- **Nostalgic / wistful**: longing for a past, with regret.
- **Dispassionate / clinical**: free of emotion, analytical.
- **Laudatory / eulogistic**: praising.
- **Conciliatory**: seeking to reduce conflict between positions.
- **Resigned**: accepting something unwelcome as unavoidable.
- **Apologetic**: in literary criticism, *defending* a position (an "apologia"), not saying sorry.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: reading disapproval from adverbs

> The ministry's new guidelines, we are assured, will "streamline" approvals. They run to 340 pages. Applicants are encouraged to consult the accompanying 90-page summary.

**The author's attitude toward the guidelines is:** (a) neutral, (b) ironic and sceptical, (c) openly hostile, (d) cautiously optimistic.

**Solution.** "We are assured" distances the author from the claim; the quotation marks around "streamline" signal doubt; the juxtaposition of "streamline" with 340 pages and a 90-page summary is ironic. There is no explicit attack, so (c) "openly hostile" overstates. (d) is the opposite. (a) ignores the irony. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Irony is detected by incongruity between a word and the facts placed next to it. Once you see the incongruity, the tone is settled and the only remaining choice is intensity.

### Example 2: describing vs endorsing

> Defenders of standardised testing argue that it is the only measure immune to the biases of individual teachers. There is something to this. A teacher's grade reflects the teacher as well as the student. But immunity to one bias is not immunity to all, and tests have biases of their own that are harder to see precisely because they are uniform.

**The author's attitude toward standardised testing is best described as:** (a) dismissive, (b) wholly supportive, (c) qualified scepticism that grants a limited merit, (d) indifferent.

**Solution.** The defenders' argument is reported, then partly endorsed ("there is something to this", plus the author's own supporting sentence), then limited by "but". So the author grants a merit and raises a stronger reservation. (a) ignores the grant; (b) ignores the "but"; (d) is wrong because the author clearly cares. Answer (c).

**Why this method.** Track three moves: report, endorse-or-reject, limit. The tone is the combination, and options that capture only one move are wrong.

### Example 3: the intensity trap

> The book's central argument is not new, and its evidence is thinner than the confident prose suggests. Still, it asks a question that the field has been avoiding, and asking it well is not nothing.

**The reviewer's tone is:** (a) scathing, (b) critical but appreciative, (c) laudatory, (d) ambivalent to the point of indecision.

**Solution.** Two criticisms (not new, thin evidence), then "still" and a real compliment (asks an avoided question, asks it well). This is moderate negative plus moderate positive: critical but appreciative. (a) is too strong; there is a compliment. (c) ignores the criticisms. (d) misreads: the reviewer is not undecided; she has a clear mixed verdict. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Place each sentence on the intensity scale. Two moderate negatives and one moderate positive do not add up to "scathing".

### Example 4: tone toward a specific object

> Earlier historians, writing in the shadow of the war, read the treaty as a failure of nerve. More recent work, with access to the negotiators' private papers, has been kinder: the treaty was the best that could be had, and its authors knew it. I find the newer reading persuasive, though it is worth remembering that private papers are written by people who expect to be judged.

**The author's attitude toward the more recent historical work is:** (a) unreserved agreement, (b) rejection, (c) agreement tempered by a methodological caution, (d) nostalgia for the earlier view.

**Solution.** "I find the newer reading persuasive" is endorsement; "though it is worth remembering… expect to be judged" is a caution about the sources it relies on. (a) drops the caution; (b) is opposite; (d) invents longing. Answer (c).

**Why this method.** The question names an object ("the more recent work"). Find every sentence about that object and ignore the rest. Here the earlier historians are a distraction.

### Example 5: dispassionate tone, critical stance

> The model assumes that households respond to a rate rise within one quarter. In the data the median lag is seven quarters. The model's forecasts for the first year after a rate rise should therefore be read as forecasts for a different economy.

**Tone?** (a) alarmed, (b) sardonic, (c) dispassionate, (d) apologetic.

**Solution.** No evaluative adjectives, no irony, no exclamation; facts and a consequence. The stance is critical (the model is wrong about timing), but the tone is dispassionate. (a) and (b) read emotion that is not there. (d) is wrong in both senses of the word. Answer (c).

**Why this method.** Separate stance from tone. A severe criticism delivered in flat prose has a dispassionate tone, and CAT likes this combination because it punishes readers who infer tone from content.

## Traps & speed tips

- **Content is not tone.** A passage about a disaster can be calm; a passage about punctuation can be furious. Read the words, not the subject.
- **Strong words in options.** *Scathing, contemptuous, reverential, euphoric* are rarely correct. Pick them only if the passage contains explicit strong language.
- **Reported view mistaken for the author's.** Check for a turn word after any "X argues that…".
- **Ambivalent vs balanced vs indifferent.** Ambivalent means torn; balanced means fair to both sides; indifferent means uninterested. They are not interchangeable.
- **Opposites.** If a passage criticises, one option will say it praises. Eliminate it first and halve your work.
- **Speed.** Note evaluative words during your first read by marking the three or four most loaded ones. Tone questions then take 30 seconds.

## Checklist

- Distinguish tone, attitude and stance, and identify the object a question asks about.
- Find evaluative adverbs, reporting verbs, hedges and irony in a passage.
- Tell describing from endorsing by checking for a turn word.
- Place a passage on the intensity scale and reject over-strong options.
- Define the twelve tone words listed above without looking.
