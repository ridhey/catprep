# Application, Strengthen & Weaken

> Two or three RC questions a year extend the passage beyond itself: "Which of the following situations best illustrates the author's point?", "Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the author's argument?", "The author would be most likely to agree with which response to…". These are the hardest RC questions to answer by memory and the easiest once you have extracted the author's principle as a rule you can apply.

## Core ideas

### What an application question tests

The passage argues something about a case; the question hands you a new case and asks what the author's argument says about it. You are not asked to recall the passage but to *run* it. That requires turning the argument into a rule.

### Step 1: extract the principle

A **principle** is the author's claim stripped of its particular subject, written as a general if–then:

- Passage: "Museum preservation is a present-day choice about what the future will be allowed to see." Principle: *Whenever someone claims to act on behalf of the future, they are exercising present power over it.*
- Passage: "Chess programs beat humans without understanding; this does not show that human chess never involved understanding." Principle: *That a task can be done without X does not show that it was never done with X.*

Write the principle in one line. If you cannot, you have not understood the argument well enough to apply it, and you should re-read the claim paragraph.

### Step 2: test each option against the principle

For "best illustrates" questions, the correct option is a new case in which the principle visibly operates: same structure, different subject. Wrong options:

- share the **subject** but not the structure (a museum story that is not about control over the future);
- share the **structure** but run it backwards (someone acting for the future who turns out to have no power over it);
- are **too general** (a restatement of the principle rather than a case).

For "the author would respond to X by…" questions, apply the principle to X and pick the option whose response follows.

### Strengthen and weaken

An argument has a **conclusion**, **evidence**, and an **assumption** (an unstated link that must hold for the evidence to support the conclusion). To strengthen or weaken, you work on the assumption.

- **Weaken**: give a fact that breaks the link. Typical moves: an alternative explanation for the evidence; a counter-example to the generalisation; a reason the evidence is unrepresentative; a reversal of cause and effect.
- **Strengthen**: give a fact that confirms the link. Typical moves: rule out an alternative explanation; add an independent case that behaves as the argument predicts; show that the evidence is representative.

A weakener does not have to refute the conclusion; it only has to make the evidence support it less. Nor does a strengthener have to prove the conclusion. Options that attack or support something the author never claimed are **out of scope**, and options that merely repeat the conclusion are **not strengtheners**: saying it again adds no support.

### Finding the assumption

Ask: "What would have to be false for this evidence to be true and the conclusion still wrong?" That is the assumption. Example: "Cash use rose after 2008, especially among the young; therefore trust, not habit, drives cash demand." Assumption: there is no other reason (apart from trust) why cash use would rise among the young after 2008. A weakener supplies one: "After 2008 many young people took informal jobs paid in cash." That explains the evidence without trust, and the argument weakens.

### Scope discipline

The most common error on these questions is picking an option that is *interesting* rather than *relevant*. Before evaluating any option, ask whether it bears on the specific link between this evidence and this conclusion. If it bears on a different claim, or on the subject generally, drop it.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: best illustrates

> We treat instruments and people by different standards: an instrument that errs is broken, a person who errs is merely a person. Our discomfort with algorithmic decisions comes from applying the instrument standard to systems that err like people.

**Principle.** *Discomfort arises when something that errs like a person is judged by the standard applied to instruments.*

**Which situation best illustrates the author's point?** (a) A calculator that gives wrong answers is returned to the shop. (b) A doctor's diagnostic software is condemned after a single misdiagnosis, though human doctors make the same error at a similar rate. (c) A new employee is forgiven for arriving late on her first day. (d) An airline replaces its pilots with an autopilot that has a lower accident rate.

**Solution.** (a) is the instrument standard applied to an instrument: no mismatch. (c) is the people standard applied to a person: no mismatch. (d) is about replacing people with a better system, which has no mismatch of standards. (b) has the structure: a system that errs like humans ("same error at a similar rate") is judged by the instrument standard ("condemned after a single misdiagnosis"). Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Options (a) and (c) share subject matter (instruments, lateness) with the passage. Only the principle, written out, shows that they lack the mismatch that the passage is about.

### Example 2: weaken

> The decline in handwriting instruction has been blamed for a decline in children's spelling. The timing supports the charge: spelling scores began falling in the same decade that schools cut handwriting hours.

**Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the argument?** (a) Handwriting instruction improves fine motor skills. (b) In the same decade, schools also replaced phonics-based reading instruction with whole-word methods. (c) Some children with excellent handwriting spell poorly. (d) Spelling scores have continued to fall in recent years.

**Solution.** Conclusion: cutting handwriting caused the spelling decline. Evidence: timing. Assumption: nothing else that changed at the same time would explain the spelling decline. (b) supplies exactly that alternative, and one directly relevant to spelling. (a) is out of scope (motor skills, not spelling). (c) is a weak counter-example that the argument, which is statistical, easily survives. (d) is consistent with the argument and does nothing to the link. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Timing arguments ("A and B happened together, so A caused B") are weakened by a third factor. Naming the assumption told you what kind of fact to look for before reading the options.

### Example 3: strengthen

Same passage. **Which, if true, would most strengthen the argument?** (a) Schools that retained handwriting hours saw no fall in spelling scores over the same decade. (b) Handwriting is valued by employers. (c) Spelling scores fell in the decade in question. (d) Teachers believe handwriting matters.

**Solution.** (c) repeats the evidence: no new support. (b) and (d) are out of scope (value and belief, not causation). (a) is a comparison that rules out "something else in that decade" as the cause: if schools that kept handwriting did not see the decline, the third-factor explanation loses force. Answer (a).

**Why this method.** A strengthener does the opposite of a weakener on the same assumption. Having found the assumption once, you use it twice.

### Example 4: how would the author respond

> Translation is a reading, and readings are judged as strong or weak, not as faithful or unfaithful. To ask whether a translation is faithful is to ask the wrong question.

**A critic complains that a new translation of a classical epic "takes liberties with the original". The author of the passage would most likely respond that:** (a) the critic is right to demand fidelity from translators of classical texts. (b) the critic should instead ask whether the translation is a strong reading of the epic. (c) all translations take liberties, so the complaint is meaningless. (d) classical epics cannot be translated.

**Solution.** Principle: *judge translations as readings (strong/weak), not as copies (faithful/unfaithful).* Apply: "takes liberties" is a fidelity complaint, so the author would redirect it to the strength question. (a) is opposite. (c) overshoots: the author says fidelity is the wrong question, not that the complaint is "meaningless" because liberties are universal. (d) is invented. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** The question is a role-play. You play the author with her principle in hand, and the correct option is what the principle says, no more.

### Example 5: an out-of-scope weakener

> The firm's internal survey shows that employees who attended the new training programme report higher job satisfaction than those who did not. The programme should therefore be made compulsory.

**Which, if true, most weakens the recommendation?** (a) The training programme is expensive. (b) Attendance at the programme was voluntary, and the employees who chose to attend were already the most engaged. (c) Job satisfaction is difficult to measure. (d) Some employees who attended later left the firm.

**Solution.** Assumption: attending the programme caused the higher satisfaction, so making everyone attend would raise everyone's satisfaction. (b) breaks it: self-selection explains the difference without the programme doing anything, so compulsion would not reproduce the effect. (a) is a cost objection, not an attack on the reasoning; it is the typical "interesting but out of scope" trap. (c) attacks all survey evidence generally and is too blunt. (d) is anecdotal and does not touch satisfaction. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** "Expensive" is a reason not to do something, but the question asks what weakens *the argument*, that is, the link between the evidence and the recommendation. Scope discipline keeps you on that link.

## Traps & speed tips

- **Subject-matching.** Options that reuse the passage's subject without its structure are the main trap on "best illustrates" questions. Write the principle without the subject, then match.
- **Repeating the conclusion.** Never a strengthener.
- **Attacking something unclaimed.** If the author did not say it, undermining it changes nothing.
- **Weakener too strong, strengthener too weak.** The right weakener is often modest (an alternative explanation); the dramatic option ("the data were fabricated") is frequently out of scope because it attacks the evidence's existence rather than its interpretation. CAT prefers the interpretive move.
- **Running the principle backwards.** Check direction: who is judging whom, what causes what.
- **Speed.** Spend the first 20 seconds writing the principle or naming the assumption. The options then take 10 seconds each.

## Checklist

- Turn any argumentative passage into a one-line, subject-free principle.
- Identify conclusion, evidence and assumption in a short argument.
- Produce a weakener by alternative explanation and a strengthener by ruling one out.
- Reject out-of-scope and conclusion-repeating options on sight.
- Answer "how would the author respond" questions by applying the principle, not by guessing the author's temperament.
