# Para-summary

> Two or three questions a year present a paragraph of 80–150 words and ask which of four options "best captures its essence". These are MCQs with negative marking, and the options are built to a formula: one correct compression and three that add, drop or distort. Knowing the formula makes the question mechanical.

## Core ideas

### What a summary is

A **summary** restates the paragraph's main claim and the reasoning that supports it, at a shorter length, without adding anything, dropping any essential step, or changing the claim's strength or direction. It is a compression, not an interpretation.

A paragraph typically has: a claim; one or two supporting reasons; sometimes a concession; sometimes an example. The summary keeps the claim and the reasons. It can drop the example (examples illustrate; they are not content). It should keep the concession *if the claim depends on it* ("X is useful but limited" is a different claim from "X is limited").

### The three ways a summary fails

CAT's wrong options are built from these:

1. **Adds.** The option includes something the paragraph does not say: a recommendation ("therefore we should…"), a cause the paragraph did not give, a generalisation beyond the paragraph's subject, an evaluation the author did not make. Test: can you point to the sentence that supports each clause of the option?

2. **Drops.** The option is true but partial: it restates one reason or the example and leaves out the claim, or restates the claim and leaves out the qualification that makes it the author's claim. Test: does the option cover the paragraph's claim *and* its main reason? Does it preserve any "but"?

3. **Distorts.** The option changes the claim: stronger ("always" for "often"), weaker ("may" for "does"), reversed (cause and effect swapped), or shifted (a different subject made the focus). Test: compare the option's verb and quantifier with the paragraph's.

### Procedure

1. Read the paragraph. Find the claim: it is usually after a "but", or the first sentence if there is no turn, or the last sentence if the paragraph builds to it.
2. Write a one-line summary yourself: "Claim, because reason, despite concession."
3. Read each option against your line. Label each wrong one: adds, drops or distorts.
4. The survivor is the answer. If two survive, compare their quantifiers and verbs with the paragraph's; one will be stronger or weaker than the text.

### Length is not a guide

The correct option is not the longest or the shortest. It is the one whose every clause is in the paragraph and whose omissions are only examples.

### "Essence" means the author's point, not the topic

An option that says what the paragraph is *about* ("the paragraph discusses the role of trust in money") is not a summary; it is a title. The summary says what the paragraph *claims* ("demand for cash rises when trust in intermediaries falls").

## Worked examples

### Example 1: add, drop, distort, right

> Standardised tests are defended as the only measure immune to the biases of individual teachers, and there is something to this: a teacher's grade reflects the teacher as well as the student. But immunity to one bias is not immunity to all. Tests have biases of their own, and because they are uniform, those biases are harder to see; a thousand teachers err in a thousand directions, while one test errs in one direction for everyone.

**Options.** (a) Standardised tests should be replaced by teacher assessment, since tests are biased. (b) Standardised tests remove teacher bias and are therefore the fairest available measure. (c) Although standardised tests avoid individual teacher bias, they carry uniform biases of their own that are harder to detect precisely because they are uniform. (d) A teacher's grade reflects the teacher as well as the student.

**Solution.** My line: tests avoid teacher bias (concession) but have their own uniform, harder-to-see biases (claim). (a) adds a recommendation and drops the concession. (b) distorts: it stops at the concession and makes it the conclusion, reversing the author's direction. (d) drops everything but one supporting sentence. (c) matches. Answer (c).

**Why this method.** Writing the line first means the options are compared with the paragraph, not with each other. Options compared with each other tempt you towards the most confident-sounding one.

### Example 2: the recommendation trap

> Firms describe lay-offs as "restructuring" and price rises as "adjustments". These words are not lies; everyone understands them. Their function is different: they move the event from the category of things someone decided to the category of things that happened, and in doing so they move responsibility out of view.

**Options.** (a) Corporate euphemisms are dishonest and should be banned. (b) Corporate euphemisms are not deceptions but devices that reclassify decisions as events, thereby hiding responsibility. (c) Everyone understands corporate euphemisms, so they do no harm. (d) Firms use words like "restructuring" to describe lay-offs.

**Solution.** (a) adds "dishonest" (the paragraph says "not lies") and "banned". (c) distorts: "everyone understands" is in the text, but "do no harm" reverses the point that they hide responsibility. (d) drops the claim and keeps the example. (b) keeps the claim (reclassify decisions as events) and its consequence (hide responsibility) and the concession (not lies). Answer (b).

**Why this method.** The option that includes "should" is wrong unless the paragraph itself prescribes. This paragraph does not.

### Example 3: the distortion of degree

> Replication failures are reported as scandals, but a result that fails to replicate has not necessarily been shown false. Many such results are true in the narrow conditions of the original study and false outside them, which is a finding about conditions, not about honesty. The scandal, where there is one, lies in the original paper's silence about how narrow the conditions were.

**Options.** (a) Replication failures are never scandals. (b) Replication failures prove that the original results were false. (c) A replication failure often reveals that a result held only under narrow conditions; the fault, if any, is the original paper's failure to say so. (d) Scientists are generally honest.

**Solution.** (a) is too strong: the paragraph says "where there is one", allowing scandals. (b) is the opposite of "not necessarily been shown false". (d) adds a general claim about honesty that the paragraph does not make. (c) preserves "many" (often), the conditions point, and the located fault. Answer (c).

**Why this method.** Check quantifiers: "never" and "prove" are both stronger than anything in the text.

### Example 4: when the claim comes last

> A city's streets were laid out when people walked; its zoning was written when they drove; its housing stock was built across both periods and belongs to neither. Each layer made sense when it was added. The incoherence we complain about is not a failure of planning but the record of a century of plans, each rational, none revised.

**Options.** (a) Cities should be replanned from scratch. (b) Urban incoherence results from successive layers of planning, each sensible at the time but never revised, rather than from an absence of planning. (c) Streets, zoning and housing were built in different periods. (d) City planning has always been irrational.

**Solution.** The claim is the last sentence. (a) adds a recommendation. (c) drops the claim and keeps the setup. (d) distorts: the paragraph says each plan was rational. (b) matches. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** When the paragraph is a build-up to a final sentence, the summary is that sentence plus the structure that led to it. Options built from the setup are "drops".

### Example 5: two survivors, decided by a verb

> The study found that participants who were told the test was timed performed worse, even when the time limit was generous enough that no one reached it. The limit did no work as a constraint; it worked as a message.

**Options.** (a) Time limits reduce performance. (b) Being told of a time limit lowered performance even when the limit was never binding, suggesting that the limit acted as a signal rather than a constraint. (c) Time limits should be removed from tests. (d) Participants who were told of a generous limit did not reach it.

**Solution.** (c) adds; (d) drops the claim. Between (a) and (b): (a) says time limits reduce performance, a general causal claim; the paragraph reports one study and a specific mechanism (the limit as message). (a) distorts by generalising and by dropping the mechanism. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** A shorter, bolder option feels like a "summary". It is usually a generalisation, which is an addition.

## Traps & speed tips

- **The option with "should".** Wrong unless the paragraph prescribes.
- **The option built from the first sentence.** Often the concession or setup; check for a "but".
- **The grand generalisation.** Shorter and bolder than the paragraph; it adds scope.
- **Dropped qualifiers.** "Often", "in part", "not necessarily" are the author's meaning. An option that drops them distorts.
- **Comparing options to each other.** Compare each to the paragraph (to your one-line summary).
- **Time.** Ninety seconds: 40 to read and write the line, 50 to label four options.

## Checklist

- Identify the claim, reasons and concession in any 100-word paragraph.
- Write a one-line summary before reading options.
- Label each wrong option as adds, drops or distorts.
- Preserve the paragraph's quantifiers and verbs in the option you choose.
- Reject recommendations and generalisations that the paragraph does not make.
