# Odd Sentence Out

> One or two questions a year give you five sentences, four of which form a paragraph and one of which does not belong; you type the number of the odd one (TITA, no negative marking). The odd sentence shares the topic with the others, so you cannot find it by subject. You find it by discovering the thread the other four share and the link the odd one lacks.

## Core ideas

### Topic vs thread

Five sentences about "memory" all share the topic. The paragraph, though, has a **thread**: a specific claim developed in a specific direction, such as "memory is reconstructive, and this has consequences for testimony". Four sentences carry the thread. The fifth is about memory but not about *that*: it may be about memory and ageing, or about how memory is stored, or it may make the opposite claim.

So the task is: find the thread, not the topic.

### How the odd sentence is built

Examiners write the odd sentence to be tempting. Common constructions:

1. **Same topic, different claim.** Four sentences argue that X; the fifth describes a feature of X unrelated to the argument.
2. **Same topic, different level.** Four sentences are general; the fifth is a narrow factual detail (a date, a statistic) that nothing in the paragraph calls for, or the reverse: four are specific and the fifth is a sweeping generalisation.
3. **Same topic, different stance.** Four sentences are critical; the fifth is approving, with no "however" in the paragraph to accommodate it.
4. **Same words, different referent.** The odd sentence uses a key word from the paragraph in a different sense.
5. **An orphan.** The sentence refers to something ("this proposal", "these results") that none of the other four supplies, and nothing in the other four refers to it.

### Procedure

1. Read the five sentences. State the topic in two words.
2. Find a **mandatory pair** among them (pronoun, connector, definite reference), exactly as in para-jumbles. Any sentence in a pair is in the paragraph.
3. Build the paragraph from the pair outward: which sentence introduces it, which follows. Usually four sentences lock together.
4. The leftover is the odd one. **Confirm** by asking: does the paragraph lose anything if this sentence is removed? And: is there any sentence that this one links to? If the answers are no and no, you are done.
5. If two sentences seem leftover, you have missed a link. Look for the one of the two that another sentence needs (a pronoun's antecedent). That one stays.

### The two confirmation tests

- **Removal test.** Delete the suspect. Read the remaining four in some order. If they form a complete, coherent paragraph, the suspect is odd.
- **Insertion test.** Try to place the suspect anywhere in the four-sentence paragraph. If every position produces a jolt (a pronoun without antecedent, a connector without contrast, a shift in claim), it is odd.

### Do not eliminate by style

The odd sentence is written in the same register as the others. Sentence length, vocabulary and formality are not evidence. Only logic (thread, reference, connectors) counts.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: same topic, different claim

> 1. Maps simplify, and the simplification is what makes them useful: a map as detailed as the territory would be as hard to read as the territory.
> 2. But every simplification is a decision about what does not matter, and that decision is made by the map-maker, not the traveller.
> 3. The earliest surviving maps were drawn on clay and show property boundaries rather than routes.
> 4. A road map treats elevation as unimportant; a hiking map treats it as the whole point.
> 5. To read a map well is therefore to ask whose purposes its silences serve.

**Solution.** Thread: maps simplify, simplification embeds the map-maker's decisions, so read maps for whose purposes they serve. Pairs: 1–2 ("But every simplification"); 2–4 (example of decisions about what matters); 4–5 or 2–5 ("therefore"). Sentence 3 is about the history and material of early maps: same topic, no link to the thread of simplification and purpose. Removal test: 1–2–4–5 is a complete paragraph. Insertion test: 3 fits nowhere without a jolt. Answer: **3**.

**Why this method.** Sentence 3 is a perfectly good sentence about maps. Only the thread, stated explicitly, shows it does not belong.

### Example 2: different level

> 1. Committees are blamed for slowness, but slowness is what they are for.
> 2. A decision that one person could take in an hour is given to a committee precisely so that it will take a month.
> 3. The month is not wasted; it is the time during which those affected can discover what is being decided and object.
> 4. The average standing committee in the civil service has eleven members.
> 5. Abolish the committee and you do not get the same decision faster; you get a different decision, taken before anyone could ask who it was for.

**Solution.** Thread: committee slowness is a feature that enables objection. 1 states it; 2 elaborates ("precisely so that"); 3 picks up "the month"; 5 draws the consequence. Sentence 4 is a statistic about committee size that nothing calls for and nothing refers to. Answer: **4**.

**Why this method.** A narrow fact with no sentence that needs it is an orphan, whatever its topic.

### Example 3: different stance with no connector to carry it

> 1. The case for open-plan offices was that visibility breeds collaboration.
> 2. What visibility actually breeds, the studies suggest, is headphones.
> 3. Workers who can be interrupted at any moment protect themselves by signalling unavailability, and the signals are more effective than walls ever were.
> 4. Open-plan layouts also reduce the cost per employee of office space, which is the reason they persist.
> 5. The collaboration moved to messaging apps, where it could be ignored at leisure.

**Solution.** Thread: open plan was meant to breed collaboration; it bred avoidance. 1 gives the case; 2 ("actually") contrasts; 3 explains the headphones; 5 says where collaboration went. Sentence 4 raises cost, a different explanation for open plan's persistence; "also" suggests it follows a benefit, but no sentence states a benefit the author endorses. It changes the subject from effect on collaboration to economics. Answer: **4**.

**Why this method.** "Also" needs a preceding point of the same kind; there is none. A connector that cannot find its partner marks the odd sentence.

### Example 4: the orphan with the wrong referent

> 1. Early modern alchemists kept meticulous records of experiments that, by their own account, never worked.
> 2. The records survive because the failures were instructive: each told the next practitioner what not to repeat.
> 3. Historians once dismissed this archive as the diary of a delusion.
> 4. They now read it as the first large body of negative results in the history of experiment.
> 5. Alchemy's goal of transmuting base metals into gold was finally shown to be impossible by nineteenth-century chemistry.

**Solution.** Thread: alchemical records of failure, once dismissed, are now valued as negative results. 1 introduces records; 2 "the records"; 3 "this archive"; 4 "they… it". Sentence 5 is about alchemy's goal and its disproof, not about the records; nothing in 1–4 refers to transmutation or the nineteenth century. Answer: **5**.

**Why this method.** Four sentences form a tight reference chain (records → the records → this archive → it). The sentence outside the chain is odd.

### Example 5: two candidates, resolved by a pronoun

> 1. Forecasts are judged by whether they came true, which is the one standard that tells you nothing about whether they were good.
> 2. A forecast of rain at 30 per cent is not wrong when it rains; it is wrong only if, over many such forecasts, it rains far more or less often than 30 per cent of the time.
> 3. This is why a single forecast can never be evaluated, only a forecaster.
> 4. Weather forecasting improved sharply after the introduction of satellite imagery in the 1960s.
> 5. Good forecasters are therefore those whose confidence matches their hit rate, not those who happened to call the one big storm.

**Solution.** Thread: evaluating forecasts by outcomes is wrong; judge calibration over many forecasts. Suspects: 4 (history of forecasting) and possibly 2 (specific example). But 3 begins "This is why", needing the explanation in 2; so 2 is in. 5's "therefore" follows 3. Sentence 4 has no sentence that needs it and refers to nothing. Answer: **4**.

**Why this method.** When two sentences look odd, the one another sentence depends on stays. Dependency beats impression.

## Traps & speed tips

- **Picking by topic word.** The odd sentence contains the topic word. Everyone's does.
- **Picking the most specific sentence.** Examples belong to paragraphs. Pick the specific sentence only if nothing calls for it.
- **Picking the sentence you understood least.** Difficulty is not oddness.
- **Not confirming.** Always run the removal test; it costs fifteen seconds and catches most errors.
- **Time.** Ninety seconds to two minutes. Pairs first, leftover second, confirm third.

## Checklist

- State the thread (not the topic) of four sentences in one line.
- Find a mandatory pair and build the paragraph outward from it.
- Apply the removal test and the insertion test to the suspect.
- Recognise the five constructions of an odd sentence (claim, level, stance, referent, orphan).
- Resolve two candidates by dependency.
