# Main Idea & Primary Purpose

> Almost every CAT passage carries one question of the form "the central idea of the passage is", "the author's primary purpose is" or "which of the following best summarises the passage". That is 3–4 questions per paper, and they are the most reliably answerable once you know the scope traps, because the wrong options are built on a fixed set of mistakes.

## Core ideas

### Main idea vs supporting detail

The **main idea** is the claim that every paragraph serves. Everything else in the passage (examples, data, quotations, the opposing view, the concession) exists to establish or sharpen that claim. A supporting detail is something that can be removed without changing what the passage argues; the main idea cannot.

Test: cover a sentence and ask "does the argument still stand?" If yes, that sentence is detail. The main idea is the sentence (or the idea behind several sentences) that, if removed, leaves the rest pointless.

### Main idea vs topic

The **topic** is the subject ("urban planning"); the main idea is a complete claim about the subject ("urban planning fails when it treats cities as machines"). An option that names the topic without making the author's claim is incomplete, and incomplete options are wrong.

### Primary purpose

"Purpose" questions ask what the author is doing, phrased with a verb: *to argue, to challenge, to describe, to explain, to reconcile, to evaluate, to question, to defend, to trace*. Two steps:

1. Pick the verb. A passage that says "the common view is X; but in fact Y" is *challenging* or *arguing*. One that lays out how something works, without taking sides, is *describing* or *explaining*. One that says "both sides have a point, and here is how they fit" is *reconciling*.
2. Check the object of the verb. "To challenge the view that clocks were neutral" is right; "to challenge the view that factories were efficient" has the right verb and the wrong object.

Most CAT passages argue, so a *describe* option is usually wrong unless the passage is genuinely expository. Conversely, a passage of pure explanation does not "argue", and an option claiming it does is too strong.

### The three scope traps

Wrong main-idea options almost always fail on scope:

- **Too narrow**: the option restates one paragraph, one example or the concession. It is true, but it is not what the whole passage is about. Signal: it mentions a specific instance (a name, a date, one case) that occupies only part of the passage.
- **Too broad**: the option is about the subject in general, beyond what the passage covers. "Technology always shapes society" when the passage is about clock time in nineteenth-century factories. Signal: words like *all, always, every, in general, throughout history* that the passage does not license.
- **Distorted**: the option covers the right territory but changes the claim: makes it stronger ("proves" for "suggests"), reverses the direction of causation, or swaps the author's view for the opposing one.

A correct main-idea option is the Goldilocks answer: it covers every paragraph, claims no more than the passage does, and preserves the author's exact direction.

### The one-line summary test

Write the main idea yourself *before* reading the options, in one line, using the map from your first read: "The passage argues that ___ , against the view that ___." Then compare each option with your line. Options are written to tempt readers who have not committed to a view; a reader with a line to compare against is rarely tempted.

### Where the main idea hides

- Not necessarily in the first paragraph; in CAT it is often in the second, after the opposing view has been stated and rejected.
- Often in the sentence after the first strong *but/however/yet*.
- Sometimes only in the last paragraph, when the author builds a case and states it at the end.
- Occasionally nowhere explicitly; you must assemble it from the concession and the rebuttal. "X is true, but it does not mean Y" has the main idea "Y does not follow from X".

## Worked examples

### Example 1: narrow, broad, distorted, right

> Museums like to say that they preserve objects for the future. Preservation, however, is a choice about what the future will be allowed to see, and that choice is made in the present by people with present interests. The nineteenth-century collector who filled European museums with Egyptian stone was not saving Egypt for posterity; he was deciding that posterity would meet Egypt in London. It is true that many objects would have been lost without such collecting. But "would have been lost" is a counterfactual of the collector's own making, and it has been used to justify a great deal. To preserve is to curate, and to curate is to govern.

**Options.** (a) Nineteenth-century collectors removed Egyptian antiquities to European museums. (b) All institutions that claim to serve the future are really serving themselves. (c) Museum preservation is not neutral safeguarding but an exercise of present-day power over what the future sees. (d) Museums should return objects because collecting never saved anything.

**Solution.** Write your own line first: "Preservation is a present-day act of control, not neutral saving, against the museum's self-description." Now compare. (a) is the Egyptian example: true, but one paragraph's support. Too narrow. (b) extends to "all institutions" and "really serving themselves"; the passage is about museums and about control, not self-interest. Too broad and distorted. (d) adds a recommendation (return objects) the passage never makes and denies the concession ("many objects would have been lost") that the author actually grants. Distorted. (c) matches the line. Answer (c).

**Why this method.** Committing to your own one-line summary before looking at options converts a recognition task (where traps work) into a matching task (where they do not).

### Example 2: primary purpose

> Economists explain the persistence of cash by habit and by the costs of switching. Both are real. Yet neither explains why cash use rose in several economies after 2008, nor why the rise was steepest among the young, who have the fewest habits and the lowest switching costs. The missing variable is trust. Cash is the one form of money whose value does not depend on the solvency of an intermediary, and demand for it rises when intermediaries look fragile.

**Options.** (a) To describe the habits that sustain cash use. (b) To argue that existing explanations of cash use overlook the role of trust. (c) To prove that young people distrust banks. (d) To reconcile the habit and switching-cost explanations.

**Solution.** Verb first. The passage says "both are real. Yet neither explains…", then supplies a missing variable: it is *arguing* that the standard account is incomplete. (a) uses the wrong verb (describe) and the wrong object (habits, which the author treats as insufficient). (c) has the wrong verb ("prove" is stronger than the passage's one piece of evidence) and the wrong object (the youth data is support, not the purpose). (d) is wrong: the author does not reconcile the two existing explanations; she adds a third. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Checking verb and object separately catches options that get one right and the other wrong, which is how purpose traps are built.

### Example 3: main idea assembled from concession and rebuttal

> That the best chess programs now beat every human is beyond dispute. What is disputed is what this shows. It plainly shows that chess can be played superbly without anything we would recognise as understanding. It does not show, as is often claimed, that understanding was never involved in human chess, any more than a car's speed shows that legs were never involved in running.

**Question.** The main point of the passage is that:

(a) chess programs lack understanding. (b) machine success at chess does not demonstrate that human chess never involved understanding. (c) cars and legs are a poor analogy for programs and humans. (d) it is disputed whether programs beat humans.

**Solution.** The structure is concession ("plainly shows… without understanding") then rebuttal ("does not show… that understanding was never involved"). The main point is the rebuttal, qualified by the concession. (a) is the concession alone: too narrow. (d) is the opposite of the first sentence. (c) is the author's own analogy misread as a target. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** When a passage is built on "X, but not Y", the main idea is "not Y", and the option that stops at X is the standard trap.

### Example 4: the "too broad" option that sounds wise

> Translation theory has spent a century arguing about fidelity, as though a translation were a copy that could be more or less exact. But a translation is a reading, and readings are not exact or inexact; they are strong or weak, generous or mean, alive or dead. To ask whether a translation is faithful is to ask the wrong question.

**Options.** (a) All interpretation is subjective, so no reading is better than another. (b) Fidelity is the wrong standard for judging translations because a translation is a reading, to be judged as readings are. (c) Translation theory has argued about fidelity for a century. (d) Translations should be judged on whether they are exact copies.

**Solution.** (a) sounds philosophical but the passage explicitly says readings can be strong or weak, so "no reading is better" contradicts it, and "all interpretation" exceeds the subject. Too broad and distorted. (c) is the setup sentence, true but not the claim. (d) is the opposite of the author's view. Answer (b).

**Why this method.** "Too broad" options often sound more profound than the passage. Profundity is not a sign of correctness; coverage is.

### Example 5: when the passage is genuinely expository

> Carbon dating works because living things take in carbon from the air, including a small, constant fraction of radioactive carbon-14, and stop doing so when they die. From that moment the carbon-14 decays at a known rate, halving every 5,730 years. Measuring how much remains therefore tells you how long ago the organism stopped exchanging carbon with its surroundings, with a precision that declines as the sample ages.

**Options.** (a) To argue that carbon dating is unreliable for old samples. (b) To explain the principle on which carbon dating rests. (c) To challenge the assumption that carbon-14 decays at a constant rate. (d) To describe the history of carbon dating.

**Solution.** No turn words, no opposing view, no evaluation; the passage explains a mechanism. Verb: *explain*. (a) distorts the final clause (precision declines) into an argument. (c) invents an opposition. (d) has the wrong object (there is no history). Answer (b).

**Why this method.** Match the verb to the passage's actual behaviour. An argumentative verb on an expository passage is as wrong as the reverse.

## Traps & speed tips

- **First-sentence trap.** The opening sentence is frequently the view the author rejects. Never pick an option that paraphrases the opening without checking for a later "but".
- **Last-sentence trap.** Striking closing lines are sometimes an implication or a flourish, not the thesis. Check that the option covers the earlier paragraphs too.
- **Example-as-thesis.** If an option names the passage's specific example (a person, a country, a date), it is almost always too narrow.
- **Quantifier creep.** Watch for *all, never, only, proves, the sole reason* in options. If the passage hedges, the correct option hedges.
- **Recommendation creep.** Options that say the author "advocates" or "calls for" something are wrong unless the passage actually prescribes.
- **Speed.** Write your one-line summary during the 30-second map, before you see any question. Main-idea questions then take under 45 seconds.

## Checklist

- Distinguish topic, main idea and supporting detail in any passage.
- Write a one-line "argues that ___, against ___" summary before reading options.
- Classify each wrong option as too narrow, too broad or distorted.
- Choose the correct purpose verb and verify its object separately.
- Recognise the "X, but not Y" structure and locate the main idea in the rebuttal.
